# Nvidia May Put $3 Billion Into SB Energy as a 10-Gigawatt OpenAI Campus Tests Who Will Finance the AI Buildout

Nvidia is discussing an investment of as much as $3 billion in SoftBank subsidiary SB Energy, which is developing a proposed 10-gigawatt Ohio data-center campus for OpenAI, according to a report by The Information summarized by Reuters on Saturday. The prospective equity investment would sit alongside roughly $100 billion in credit support being discussed for the project—turning what appears at first to be a semiconductor story into a test of how the technology industry intends to finance infrastructure whose costs have expanded beyond what even the largest AI companies can comfortably fund themselves.

The talks are not final, and Reuters said it could not independently verify The Information’s report. Nvidia and SB Energy did not immediately comment. But the proposed structure is unusually revealing: Nvidia could invest roughly half of the $3 billion when the Ohio agreement is signed and the remainder as part of an SB Energy initial public offering that could occur as soon as next month and raise at least $5 billion.

## AI’s bottleneck is moving from chips to capital

For much of the generative-AI boom, Nvidia’s challenge was producing enough accelerators to meet demand. The next constraint increasingly appears financial. Data centers require not just GPUs but land, electricity generation, transmission infrastructure, cooling systems, networking equipment and buildings capable of supporting extraordinary power density.

That has pushed the financing requirements into a category historically associated with power plants, highways and telecommunications networks rather than software companies. The proposed Ohio campus would reach 10 gigawatts if fully completed and would be the largest announced data-center project to date, according to reporting cited by Reuters. OpenAI is discussing a binding lease for the full site.

OpenAI’s scale complicates the credit question. Reuters reported the company remains unprofitable despite an estimated $852 billion valuation. A private valuation can make a company enormously valuable on paper without giving lenders the same assurance as stable operating cash flow or an investment-grade credit rating. For infrastructure lenders funding assets over decades, that distinction matters.

That is where Nvidia enters a role far removed from merely selling processors. A financial guarantee from one of the world’s most cash-generative technology companies can make a project easier and cheaper to finance because lenders gain recourse to a stronger balance sheet if the primary borrower or tenant cannot fulfill its commitments.

## Nvidia has already reduced how much risk it may take

The scale of Nvidia’s contemplated support has already changed. The Wall Street Journal reported that Nvidia is expected initially to guarantee less than $120 billion for the Ohio project, down from as much as $250 billion previously discussed. The reduction followed investor concern about how much financial exposure Nvidia would assume by backstopping other companies’ AI infrastructure commitments, according to Reuters’ summary of the Journal reporting.

That does not mean Nvidia is retreating from AI financing. Days earlier, the company announced partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR intended to mobilize more than $500 billion of third-party capital for AI infrastructure. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang said the company could backstop up to $125 billion, or 25%, of potential transactions under those financing platforms.

The shift is therefore from Nvidia carrying enormous project-specific exposure itself toward a model in which institutional investors, private-equity firms, infrastructure funds and banks provide a larger portion of the capital. That resembles mature infrastructure finance more than traditional venture funding. It also distributes the risk. If AI demand continues rising, investors gain exposure to long-lived computing assets and usage-linked revenue. If demand disappoints or hardware generations become obsolete faster than lenders expect, those same assets could prove less valuable than their financing assumptions anticipated.

## The supplier is becoming part financier

The structure creates an unusual relationship. Nvidia sells the chips that many AI developers need; those developers need increasingly expensive data centers to deploy the chips; Nvidia can help finance those data centers, which in turn generates additional demand for Nvidia products.

There is nothing inherently unprecedented about suppliers financing customers. Aircraft manufacturers, industrial-equipment companies and automakers have long used financing to expand the pool of buyers able to purchase expensive products. What is unusual is the scale. Nvidia said the six-institution financing initiative is aimed at more than half a trillion dollars in third-party capital, while Big Tech companies collectively are expected to spend more than $730 billion on AI-related infrastructure this year.

That circularity is economically powerful when end demand keeps expanding. Financing makes more computing capacity possible; additional capacity enables more AI services; successful AI services generate the revenue needed to service the financing.

It also means investors increasingly need to evaluate AI infrastructure as a complete economic chain rather than judging Nvidia, OpenAI or a data-center developer independently. A weakness at one stage can propagate into the others.

## Electricity may ultimately matter as much as computing

SB Energy’s role underscores another change. Founded in 2019 and backed by SoftBank and OpenAI, the company develops large-scale power and data-center infrastructure rather than AI models. Its emergence near the center of an Nvidia-OpenAI transaction shows how electricity procurement is becoming inseparable from AI strategy.

A 10-gigawatt facility would require power on the scale of a major metropolitan area. Obtaining that electricity means securing generation, transmission access, backup systems and long-term regulatory approvals—tasks fundamentally different from writing software or designing chips.

For the AI industry, that produces a new competitive advantage: access to capital and power may matter almost as much as model quality. A laboratory can possess leading researchers and still be constrained if it cannot finance the equipment and electricity required to run its systems at global scale.

The Ohio project remains proposed rather than completed, and the Nvidia investment remains under discussion. Those qualifications are essential because the numbers involved are so large that announcements can easily be mistaken for deployed capital.

What is already clear is the financing architecture forming around it. Nvidia is moving beyond the position of a component supplier, private capital firms are treating compute as an infrastructure asset class, power developers are becoming strategic AI partners, and model companies are signing obligations whose scale would once have been associated with national infrastructure programs. The next phase of the AI race may therefore be decided not only by who builds the best model or fastest chip, but by who can assemble the balance sheets capable of keeping millions of those chips powered.
