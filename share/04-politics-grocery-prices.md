# Prices Across 26 Grocery Categories Are Up 3.4% Since Trump Returned to Office as Republicans Lose a Decade-Long Polling Edge on the Economy

Prices across 26 grocery categories displayed by Donald Trump during his August 2024 campaign attack on inflation have risen an average 3.4% during the first 19 months of his second presidency, according to a Reuters analysis of Bureau of Labor Statistics data. The increase is less than one-quarter the pace recorded over the comparable opening period of Joe Biden’s presidency, but it also demonstrates a politically consequential distinction: inflation has slowed substantially, while the overall price level has generally not moved backward.

That distinction now sits near the center of the 2026 midterms. A Reuters/Ipsos poll found 37% of registered voters preferred the Democratic Party’s approach to the economy and 36% preferred the Republican approach, the first Democratic advantage on that question in nearly a decade. Separately, Democrats led 42% to 37% on the generic congressional ballot in that poll, although national polling cannot predict outcomes in individual House districts or Senate races.

## Inflation is slowing, but lower inflation does not mean lower prices

The latest official CPI report gives both parties evidence they can emphasize. Overall consumer prices increased 3.4% over the 12 months ending in July, down from 3.5% in June. Grocery prices, measured by the BLS food-at-home index, rose 2.7% year over year but actually declined 0.1% from June to July. Core inflation excluding food and energy stood at 2.5%.

The problem for anyone promising broad price reductions is arithmetic. When inflation falls from a high rate to a low rate, prices generally continue increasing—just more slowly. A family that watched a grocery item rise from $4 to $5 during one period does not see the item return to $4 merely because its subsequent annual inflation rate falls to 2%. For voters evaluating their purchasing power, the relevant comparison is often today’s checkout total against what they remember paying several years ago, rather than the latest monthly change.

Individual groceries also tell dramatically different stories. Egg prices have fallen nearly 40% since Trump returned to office, largely reversing the spike associated with avian-flu shortages that began during the Biden administration. Six of Reuters’ 26 categories declined, including butter, bacon and cooking fats and oils. At the other end, uncooked beef rose 19.2%, apples 14%, fresh fish and seafood 8.6%, and coffee nearly 23%.

The broader CPI shows similar divergence. Food-at-home prices were up 2.7% year over year in July, but energy costs were up 14.7%, including a 24.6% increase in gasoline. Prescription-drug prices fell 0.8% just in July, while airline fares were 25.5% higher than a year earlier. “The cost of living” is therefore not one price trajectory but the combined effect of dozens of categories with different causes.

## The political shift is real, but the margin remains narrow

Republicans held a durable advantage over Democrats on economic management for much of the last decade, making the current 37%-to-36% Reuters/Ipsos result notable. But a one-point advantage is not evidence that voters have broadly embraced one party’s economic program over the other’s; 27% selected neither, another party or were unsure, and the poll’s margin of error means the parties are effectively close.

Cost-of-living polling is less ambiguous. Ipsos reported this month that 35% preferred Democrats on handling the cost of living compared with 27% who preferred Republicans, an eight-point gap. Reuters separately reported Trump’s net approval on cost-of-living management at minus 47 percentage points, compared with minus 35 on the broader economy and minus 16 on immigration.

Those figures are affecting campaign strategy, particularly in competitive suburban districts. Reuters interviewed Republican and independent voters in New Jersey’s 7th Congressional District, where Republican Rep. Tom Kean faces Democrat Rebecca Bennett. Some Trump voters said food and gasoline costs had reduced their enthusiasm, while others said border security, education or ideological concerns remained more important and that they continued to support Republican candidates. Kean’s campaign did not respond to Reuters’ request for comment for that report; Bennett said she favors reversing tariffs, reducing U.S. involvement in the Iran war and restoring food-assistance benefits.

## Presidents matter to inflation, but they do not control it

Assigning all price movements to a president—either Biden previously or Trump now—overstates presidential control. Monetary policy is set independently by the Federal Reserve, while commodity prices, wars, weather, disease outbreaks, wages, housing supply, global shipping and private-sector pricing decisions all affect inflation. Reuters explicitly noted that presidents have limited direct power over the overall inflation rate.

Presidential policies can still matter. Tariffs can change import costs; fiscal policy can affect aggregate demand; energy and foreign-policy decisions can influence fuel markets; regulations can raise or lower business costs; and appointments to agencies can alter longer-run conditions. The analytical problem is separating those effects from shocks that would have occurred under either party.

The egg market is an unusually clear example. Prices fell sharply during Trump’s current term, but much of that decline reflects recovery from a supply shock caused by avian influenza rather than a single White House action. Gasoline provides the reverse problem: prices have risen sharply amid the Iran conflict and energy-market disruption, but oil and gasoline prices are set in global markets where presidential decisions interact with events and producers beyond U.S. control.

## November may turn on the difference between economic data and household memory

The White House points to the cooler July CPI report, lower prices in categories such as eggs and prescription drugs, and the much slower pace of food inflation compared with the first portion of Biden’s term. Those are measurable improvements in the inflation rate and in specific products. Critics point to the equally measurable fact that many absolute prices remain above their January 2025 levels and that gasoline, beef, coffee and other frequently purchased items have risen substantially.

Both observations can be true simultaneously. Inflation is lower than it was during the earlier post-pandemic surge. Many prices are also higher than they were when Trump promised voters that costs would come down.

That distinction may be why grocery prices have become politically durable. Gross domestic product, employment reports and inflation rates are abstractions most voters encounter through news coverage. Food prices are observed directly, repeatedly and personally. As Berkeley public-policy professor Francesco Trebbi told Reuters, grocery shopping creates a recurring reminder of household purchasing power. Whether that frustration ultimately shifts congressional control remains uncertain, but the economic advantage Republicans carried into Trump’s second term is no longer visible in current national polling.
