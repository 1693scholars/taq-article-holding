# German Investment in the U.S. Falls Nearly Two-Thirds to €4.3 Billion — but Companies Already Here Are Still Reinvesting Their Profits

German companies cut direct investment in the United States to €4.3 billion, or roughly $5 billion, during the first half of 2026, a decline of nearly two-thirds from a year earlier and the lowest first-half level since 2023, according to German Economic Institute calculations based on Bundesbank data reviewed by Reuters. Compared with the first half of 2024, German investment has fallen almost 80%.

The headline number points to a significant pullback in new capital commitments, but the composition of the data prevents a simpler conclusion that German companies are abandoning the American market. Researchers found that businesses already operating in the United States continued to reinvest unusually large amounts of their U.S. profits, while fresh equity investment remained below average. In other words, the installed base of German corporate America still sees value in being here; the hesitation is concentrated in decisions about committing additional capital.

## The decline is large even before politics enters the calculation

In the five years before the COVID-19 pandemic, German companies invested an average of €15.8 billion in the United States during the first half of each year—almost four times the 2026 figure. The pandemic then distorted cross-border capital flows sufficiently that German Economic Institute researcher Samina Sultan cautioned against treating every subsequent year as directly comparable; some pandemic-era periods even produced net investment outflows.

Even with that caveat, €4.3 billion represents a clear change in direction. Direct investment is distinct from buying publicly traded U.S. stocks or bonds. It typically reflects longer-lived corporate commitments—factories, subsidiaries, acquisitions, equipment, intercompany financing and retained earnings intended to support operations. Those investments are harder to reverse than portfolio investments, which is precisely why businesses tend to delay them when the rules governing future costs are uncertain.

The German Economic Institute links the decline partly to uncertainty surrounding U.S. trade policy since President Donald Trump returned to office in January 2025. The administration has used or threatened tariffs against trading partners as leverage to obtain trade concessions, while the European Union negotiated a deal last year that included a $600 billion investment pledge designed in part to avoid heavier U.S. duties.

That attribution should be distinguished from proof that tariffs alone caused the decline. Investment decisions also respond to interest rates, energy costs, exchange rates, demand projections, labor availability, corporate balance sheets and conditions in Germany itself. The data establish that German investment has fallen sharply during a period of heightened trade-policy uncertainty; they do not isolate a single variable responsible for every euro that was not invested.

## Existing operations tell a different story

The strongest evidence against interpreting the figures as a wholesale rejection of the United States comes from the investment breakdown itself. German companies generated high levels of reinvested earnings and direct-investment lending during 2025. What remained weak was narrower equity capital—the balance created when businesses put new ownership capital into U.S. ventures or liquidate existing positions.

“Companies that are already active in the United States are therefore continuing to reinvest the profits they earn there in the country,” Sultan told Reuters, adding that the pattern suggests the United States “remains an attractive market overall.” Her conclusion was narrower on new investment: companies appear hesitant to commit fresh capital.

That distinction matters for states competing to attract German manufacturing. Germany is home to major automobile, chemical, industrial-equipment, pharmaceutical and engineering companies with large American operations. Once a factory, dealer network, supplier base or distribution system exists, reinvesting earnings can remain economically rational even during periods when management postpones an entirely new plant.

The concern for U.S. economic development is therefore not principally that existing German employers will disappear overnight. It is that the next factory, research center or supply-chain expansion could be delayed, downsized or located elsewhere while executives wait for greater clarity about the trade relationship.

## Tariffs can alter investment in opposite directions

Trade barriers create an unusual two-sided incentive for foreign manufacturers. A tariff on imported goods can make exporting into the United States less attractive, which in theory encourages a manufacturer to build more production inside the country to avoid the tariff. That is one of the arguments frequently made for using tariffs as an industrial-policy tool.

At the same time, tariffs imposed across complex international supply chains can make U.S. production more expensive if an American factory itself depends on imported components, machinery or raw materials. Uncertainty over whether tariff rates will rise, fall or be replaced through negotiation can also make calculating the expected return on a 20-year plant more difficult. The same policy can therefore encourage localization for one company while delaying investment for another, depending on its supply chain.

The German data are notable because the aggregate result so far is not a surge in fresh capital designed to get behind tariff barriers. New commitments have weakened, while already-established operations continue reinvesting. That pattern is consistent with companies preserving businesses they know while exercising more caution toward projects they have not yet begun.

## The real test will be whether delayed investment becomes canceled investment

Six months of data cannot establish a permanent relocation of German capital. Large industrial projects are irregular: a handful of factory investments or acquisitions can materially change annual totals, and the pandemic demonstrated how unusual events can distort cross-border comparisons. The Institute itself emphasizes those limitations.

What makes the 2026 number consequential is its combination of magnitude and composition. German investment is running far below both recent levels and the pre-pandemic norm, yet German companies already established in America continue to recycle profits into their U.S. operations. That produces a more precise diagnosis than either “foreign companies are fleeing” or “nothing has changed.”

The United States remains profitable enough for many German companies to keep reinvesting. It has become uncertain enough, according to the institute examining the data, that those same companies are substantially less willing to make new commitments. Whether that hesitation becomes a lasting change in transatlantic capital flows will depend on what happens after the current trade rules become clearer—and whether the projects now being postponed eventually move forward somewhere else.
