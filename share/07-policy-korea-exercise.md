# Trump Orders an Exercise With 18,000 South Korean Troops Scaled Back Hours Before It Starts, Reopening a Deterrence-for-Diplomacy Tradeoff

President Donald Trump ordered the Pentagon to “substantially reduce” the annual U.S.–South Korean Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise hours before it was scheduled to begin, citing its cost, what he called an unnecessarily hostile signal toward North Korea and Seoul’s decision not to participate in U.S. military operations against Iran. The August 17–27 exercise involves about 18,000 South Korean troops alongside American forces and began Monday as scheduled while military officials worked to determine what the president’s directive would change.

The decision does not alter the formal U.S.–South Korea security alliance or the roughly 28,500 American service members stationed in South Korea. It does, however, revive a policy dispute that has followed Trump since his first presidency: whether reducing major military exercises can create diplomatic space with North Korea without materially weakening the readiness of forces assigned to deter an attack.

## The exercise was designed around threats North Korea is actively developing

Ulchi Freedom Shield is one of two principal annual combined exercises conducted by the U.S. and South Korean militaries. This year’s training was expected to include counter-drone operations, responses to GPS interference and cyberattacks, precision targeting, live-fire maneuver, wet-gap crossings and the distribution of prepositioned equipment. Those scenarios reflect changes in North Korea’s capabilities rather than a static replay of Cold War training.

North Korean troops that fought alongside Russia in Ukraine have gained exposure to modern battlefield conditions involving unmanned aircraft, electronic warfare and cyber operations, U.S. military officials have said. Pyongyang has also resumed ballistic-missile launches, firing missiles on August 6 and again days before the exercise began.

From the alliance’s military perspective, the value of the exercise is interoperability: making sure American and South Korean units can communicate, move equipment, coordinate targeting and operate under attack as one force rather than two separate militaries. U.S. military statements have consistently described Ulchi Freedom Shield as defensive training intended to strengthen combined readiness and deterrence. Previous Defense Department descriptions similarly characterize the exercise as all-domain training conducted in support of the bilateral alliance.

## Trump sees the same exercise through a diplomatic lens

North Korea describes the drills very differently, routinely characterizing them as rehearsals for invasion. Pyongyang issued another warning before this year’s exercise and threatened unspecified countermeasures. Such statements are longstanding features of the peninsula’s annual military cycle, often accompanied by missile tests or other weapons demonstrations.

Trump has argued since his first term that large exercises are both expensive and provocative. After his 2018 summit with Kim Jong Un in Singapore, he surprised U.S. and South Korean officials by announcing plans to stop what he called “war games” while negotiations proceeded. The two leaders ultimately met three times, but those talks did not produce an agreement ending North Korea’s nuclear-weapons or ballistic-missile programs.

The current decision appears to return to the same theory of diplomacy: reducing activities Pyongyang considers threatening may create conditions more conducive to direct negotiations. Trump specifically cited his “very good relationship” with Kim and wrote that the exercises send a signal he considers inappropriate toward a North Korea that he described as respectful during his presidency.

Jenny Town, who heads the Stimson Center’s 38 North program, told Reuters the move may be intended to create a diplomatic opportunity with Pyongyang, though she questioned whether the abrupt manner of the announcement would produce much effect there.

## The Iran reference adds a separate alliance question

Trump also tied his decision to South Korea’s refusal to participate in U.S. actions aimed at Iran’s nuclear program. He acknowledged in his own post that the issue was “somewhat unrelated,” but cited Seoul’s response as part of his dissatisfaction with proceeding with the exercise.

That linkage broadens the policy question beyond North Korea. Washington’s alliances are generally structured around defined geographic security commitments, and South Korea’s treaty relationship with the United States is principally centered on the Korean Peninsula. Whether an ally should be expected to participate in a separate Middle Eastern conflict is therefore a political question distinct from its contribution to peninsula defense.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth had praised South Korea only months earlier for increasing defense spending and assuming greater responsibility for its own security, calling Seoul’s efforts an example of alliance burden-sharing. That record makes the current disagreement more specific than a simple claim that South Korea is not contributing to its defense relationship.

## Readiness and diplomacy are not mutually exclusive, but they compete for the same signal

Critics of reducing the exercise argue that military readiness degrades when units cannot practice at realistic scale. Democratic Sen. Mark Kelly, a former Navy pilot, called the order shortsighted. The military case is particularly focused on combined operations: individual American and South Korean units can train separately, but that does not fully reproduce the command, logistics and communications problems that emerge when tens of thousands of personnel operate together.

The administration’s countervailing calculation is that deterrence itself can become destabilizing if the other side interprets an exercise as preparation for attack. Reducing a drill can therefore function as a diplomatic signal without withdrawing troops or changing treaty obligations.

Both mechanisms are real. Exercises can improve preparedness, and adversaries can perceive them as threatening. The policy dispute concerns how to balance those effects under current conditions.

The unanswered question Monday was what “substantially reduce” means operationally. The South Korean military confirmed the exercise had started but did not immediately explain which components would be canceled or modified. The Pentagon referred questions about implementation to the White House.

That uncertainty may ultimately matter as much as the decision itself. A planned, negotiated reduction can be incorporated into alliance readiness schedules and used deliberately as diplomatic leverage. An order issued hours before an exercise begins forces commanders to alter a complex multinational training event while it is already in motion.

Trump’s directive therefore reopens a familiar argument without resolving it: whether military exercises are primarily an instrument for preparing to deter North Korea, or a bargaining chip that can sometimes be reduced in order to engage it. The United States has tried both approaches over the past decade. North Korea’s nuclear and missile capabilities have continued advancing through both.
