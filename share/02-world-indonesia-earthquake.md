# Indonesia’s 7.7 Earthquake Kills at Least 54 and Damages 1,300 Homes as Nearly 1,600 Aftershocks Keep Thousands Outside

At least 54 people have died, 135 have been injured and about 5,000 have been forced into temporary shelter after a magnitude-7.7 earthquake struck Indonesia’s East Nusa Tenggara province, according to the latest Associated Press reporting, while Reuters reported that the region had registered nearly 1,600 aftershocks by Monday. More than 1,300 homes have been damaged, roads remain obstructed by landslides and some communities on Flores Island were still waiting for basic aid as Indonesia marked its 81st Independence Day.

The earthquake struck shortly before 6 a.m. Saturday at a shallow depth of about 10 kilometers, with its epicenter off Flores near Ende, according to the U.S. Geological Survey and Indonesian authorities cited by the AP. The province’s governor has declared a 14-day state of emergency, allowing additional resources to be mobilized while residents face the simultaneous problems of damaged housing, continuing aftershocks, disrupted transportation and fear of returning indoors.

## A shallow earthquake transferred its energy directly into populated areas

Magnitude alone does not determine an earthquake’s destructiveness. Depth, distance from populated areas, local geology and building quality all determine how severely energy at the fault translates into movement at the surface. At roughly 10 kilometers deep, the Flores quake was shallow enough to produce intense ground motion across communities whose housing stock includes structures poorly equipped for prolonged lateral shaking.

Landslides compounded the direct structural damage. Reuters reported blocked roads in Sikka and Manggarai, while AP journalists saw fallen trees, large boulders thrown down slopes and crushed vehicles beneath collapsed buildings. In the first days of the response, some of the hardest-hit districts were inaccessible or difficult to reach because road and communications failures occurred at the same time rescuers needed them most.

The damage assessments remain fluid. AP, citing Indonesia’s National Disaster Management Agency, reported more than 1,300 damaged homes, including nearly 250 destroyed on Flores. Reuters cited disaster-agency chief Suharyanto saying roughly 500 homes had been damaged in the areas assessed at that point. Such differences are common during large disasters because agencies update totals at different times and may use different definitions for damaged, severely damaged and destroyed structures.

## Aftershocks have become a disaster within the disaster

Authorities had recorded at least 995 aftershocks in an earlier AP count; by Monday, Reuters reported the head of Indonesia’s geophysics agency saying the total was approaching 1,600, including a new magnitude-5.6 event near the original epicentral region. Thousands of residents are sleeping outside, not solely because their homes are destroyed but because even apparently intact buildings may be unsafe until inspected.

That changes the logistical requirements of the response. A household whose house remains standing may nevertheless need a tent, sanitation, drinking water and food because entering the structure is considered too dangerous. Medical facilities face the same problem: Reuters described patients being treated in tents outside a damaged health center, while the government said emergency field hospitals would be established and that neurosurgeons and orthopedic surgeons were needed for head injuries and fractures.

Indonesia has deployed more than 3,500 military and police personnel and sent approximately 275 tons of relief supplies into the affected region, according to the AP. Yet survivors in some communities continued to report shortages of drinking water, medicine, food and cooking equipment. That gap illustrates a recurring disaster-response problem in archipelagic countries: national authorities can possess substantial relief stocks while damaged ports, roads and local communications prevent those supplies from reaching the final kilometers where they are needed.

## Flores has experienced this combination before

The earthquake has particular resonance on Flores because the island suffered a destructive earthquake and tsunami in 1992 that killed roughly 2,500 people. Saturday’s quake initially triggered tsunami warnings that sent residents toward higher ground before authorities lifted the alert. The memory of prior disasters, combined with continuing aftershocks, helps explain why many residents remain unwilling to return to coastal homes even after the immediate tsunami threat passed.

Indonesia’s geography makes earthquakes routine but does not make their consequences routine. The country sits across the Pacific Ring of Fire, an arc of active faults and volcanoes that has produced some of the world’s most destructive seismic disasters. The 2004 Sumatra earthquake and Indian Ocean tsunami killed about 230,000 people across multiple countries, while Indonesia’s 2018 Sulawesi earthquake and tsunami killed more than 4,400, according to AP historical reporting.

Saturday’s earthquake is already Indonesia’s deadliest since the 2022 West Java disaster. What remains uncertain is how much the toll will change as road access improves and damage assessments reach communities that were initially isolated. Reuters reported Monday that rescue teams planned to continue aerial searches and use helicopters and extraction equipment, even though officials had no confirmed list of missing people at that point.

For now, the most consequential number may be the thousands living outside rather than the official casualty count. They represent a population whose exposure continues after the principal earthquake: damaged houses, unstable hillsides, limited sanitation, disrupted medical care and hundreds of aftershocks a day. The shaking that lasted seconds has therefore created a recovery problem likely to last months, with the first challenge simply determining which buildings, roads and communities are safe enough to reoccupy.
