# Congo’s Ebola Death Toll Reaches 2,325 as 60–70% of New Cases Appear Outside Contact Monitoring

The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has killed 2,325 people among 4,945 confirmed cases, surpassing the country’s 2018–2020 epidemic to become the deadliest Ebola outbreak in Congolese history, according to government figures reported Sunday by Reuters. Authorities detected 101 additional confirmed infections in the preceding 24 hours, while the case-fatality rate has climbed to roughly 46%—meaning nearly one of every two confirmed patients has died.

The most consequential statistic may be outside the mortality count. Between 60% and 70% of newly identified patients were not already being monitored as known contacts, according to health workers and officials interviewed by the Associated Press. That means the surveillance system designed to find exposed people before they become severely ill is missing most new infections until after transmission opportunities have already occurred.

## This outbreak is moving differently from Congo’s recent Ebola epidemics

Congo has experienced Ebola repeatedly since the virus was first identified in 1976, and the current epidemic is its 17th outbreak. Yet this one is caused by the rarer Bundibugyo species, for which no approved vaccine or specific treatment currently exists. WHO has designated the outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern and says cross-border transmission has already reached Uganda.

By August 9, WHO had documented 4,381 confirmed cases and 2,011 deaths, with 85.8% of cases and 80.6% of deaths concentrated in Ituri Province. The outbreak crossed 2,000 deaths only 86 days after it was formally declared, an acceleration far faster than Congo’s previous epidemics. Updated national figures reported a week later pushed the toll past 2,300.

Only the 2014–2016 West African Ebola epidemic remains larger globally. That outbreak produced 28,616 cases and 11,310 deaths across Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone. Congo’s current outbreak has not approached those cumulative totals, but it reached major mortality milestones substantially faster, prompting WHO officials to warn that continued uncontrolled growth could eventually challenge the West African record.

## A rising fatality rate does not mean the virus has become more deadly

The case-fatality ratio has risen from about 20% in early June to approximately 46%, according to government data cited by Reuters. That could superficially suggest a virus becoming biologically more lethal. Public-health specialists say the more likely explanation is operational failure: patients are being identified later in illness, when supportive care has less opportunity to prevent death.

Thomas Parisch, a public-health specialist deployed with Médecins Sans Frontières, told Reuters that fatality rates would ordinarily be expected to fall as an outbreak progresses because contact tracing improves and infected patients enter treatment earlier. Instead, health teams are still identifying many patients only after severe deterioration or death in the community.

That explanation is consistent with the contact-tracing data. If 60% to 70% of new cases arise among people who were never on surveillance lists, health workers cannot check them regularly for fever or other symptoms, isolate them promptly, or trace their contacts before additional transmission occurs.

“Surveillance is the engine of a response,” Congolese surveillance officer Gédéon Banga Ngbape told the AP. His teams in Nizi were receiving 60 to 70 alerts per day, sometimes working from 8 a.m. until close to midnight. Yet frontline workers described shortages of boots, disinfectant and protective supplies; some said they purchased disinfectant themselves.

## The response is colliding with payroll, conflict and geography

Some Ebola-response workers have gone unpaid for more than three months, contributing to strikes and temporary treatment-center closures. Congo’s government says payments were delayed while it verified payroll lists after discovering unrelated names and transitioned from cash payments to mobile money. Africa CDC chief Jean Kaseya has urged authorities to resolve the wage problem, arguing that funds are available and workers performing dangerous response duties need to be paid.

Those administrative problems sit on top of eastern Congo’s security crisis. Armed groups operate across Ituri and neighboring regions, population displacement is constant, mining communities are highly mobile and some roads become impassable. WHO describes the outbreak as occurring amid insecurity, refugee movements and substantial cross-border trade, all conditions that make maintaining a stable contact list unusually difficult.

Community distrust adds another layer. Health authorities have documented misinformation asserting that Ebola does not exist, resistance to restrictions on traditional burial practices and attacks on some health facilities. Such resistance is not merely a communications problem: Ebola remains infectious in bodily fluids after death, making safe burials a core part of transmission control.

## Medicine is racing to catch a strain it was not prepared for

Unlike the Zaire Ebola species responsible for several previous epidemics, Bundibugyo virus disease currently has no licensed vaccine or approved targeted therapy. Two candidate vaccines are being tested in humans, and WHO is also evaluating whether an existing Ebola vaccine might provide cross-protection after encouraging animal studies. Experimental therapeutics are being investigated as well, but none can yet substitute for basic outbreak control.

That leaves the oldest tools—early detection, isolation, infection control, contact tracing, supportive care, safe burial and community cooperation—as the primary defense. The paradox is that those tools require exactly what eastern Congo has in shortest supply: reliable staffing, transportation, security, trust and sustained local health infrastructure.

The outbreak’s record death toll is therefore not only a measure of the virus. It is also a measure of the distance between a known public-health strategy and the system available to execute it.

Congo has more Ebola-response experience than almost any country in the world. Yet experience cannot compensate indefinitely for an outbreak in which most new infections are emerging outside existing surveillance, frontline workers lack equipment or pay, armed conflict limits access, and the viral species lacks the approved countermeasures used against other Ebola strains. Until that surveillance gap closes, the epidemic’s most important trend is likely to remain the one health workers are already describing: they are finding the virus after it has moved, rather than before.
